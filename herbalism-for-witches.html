Herbalism for
Witches

How to start working with plants safely.

Herbalism  •  Beginner  •  October 2026

If you've ever tucked a bay leaf into your wallet, sprinkled rosemary across a doorway, or slept with mugwort under your pillow, you've already practiced plant magick. Herbs are one of the oldest tools in the craft, and for a lot of witches, they're the heart of it.

They're also one of the easiest places to make a mistake, because plants are real substances with real effects on your body. So let's talk about what herbal magick actually is, where it comes from, and how to start working with plants in a way that's both powerful and safe.

What an Herb Actually Is

At its simplest, an herb is a plant whose fragrance or taste lends itself to cooking, healing, or perfumery. The word usually refers to the parts that grow aboveground, mainly the leaves and flowers. In gardening terms, an herb is a plant with a soft, nonwoody stem.

Spices fall under the same umbrella. They're usually the dried seeds, bark, or roots of aromatic plants, and they tend to come from warmer subtropical regions.

In practice, though, any part of a plant can serve a magickal purpose: leaves, flowers, roots, bark, resin, and seeds. And different parts can carry different energies. Rose petals, rose hips, and rose thorns aren't interchangeable, so always check which part a working calls for.

A Very Long History

People have used herbs to ease pain and treat illness since ancient times, passing remedies down through generations of trial and error. Monasteries were built around herb gardens that became some of the earliest pharmacies and hospitals. At the same time, wise women tended their own gardens and foraged hedgerows and woodlands, developing remedies of their own.

The Breton word for herb, louzaouenn, is also the word for medicine. Anyone who knew plants well could quite literally kill or cure, and they were respected for it.

A few names are worth knowing.

Hildegard von Bingen (1098 to 1179) was a nun, visionary, and healer whose medical writings came out of her own monastic garden. She connected a person's health to the health of the world around them and wove in the four elements, directions, and seasons.

Nicholas Culpeper translated Latin medical knowledge into English so ordinary people could use herbal remedies, and he treated the poor of London when disease was rampant. He also strongly believed in astrology and linked herbs to the planets. Many of the planetary associations witches still use today trace back to his work.

The Lacnunga, a manuscript from the tenth or eleventh century written in Latin and Old English, records herbalism practiced with magickal intent, including enchanting herbs with invocations and charms. That pairing of words of power with deliberate action is considered a forerunner of modern spellcasting.

Many herbs were also given secret witch names. These coded names protected a spell's ingredients from being discovered, and they protected the witch. If no one could identify what was in a working, its maker was far less likely to be persecuted. Using those names today is a way to honor the people who needed them.

How Plant Correspondences Work

Every plant carries its own correspondences, the energetic and symbolic associations, linking it to certain intentions, planets, elements, and deities. When rosemary shows up in a protection working, you're calling on its long history in purification, its solar associations, and its connection to memory and clarity.

A lot of those associations started with the Doctrine of Signatures, the old belief that a plant affects whatever it resembles. It's the same idea behind the Law of Similars, the principle that like affects like. The pomegranate and the tomato became fertility symbols because of their abundant seeds. The pansy, also called heartsease, was said to cheer the heart because of the shape of its leaves.

But here's an important warning: the rule doesn't always hold. Mistletoe was nicknamed "all heal," yet the plant is poisonous. Hemlock looks so much like parsley that it earned the name "poison parsley." Resemblance can point you toward a plant's symbolism. It can never tell you whether a plant is safe.

Plants also connect to the planets, which gives you another way in. Venus plants for love, Mars plants for courage, Saturn plants for protection. And if a working calls for an herb you don't have, look for another plant that shares the same correspondences. If you're missing mugwort for dream work, any plant linked to dreams, psychic work, and the moon can step in. Dried herbs work just as well as fresh for symbolic work.

Ways to Work with Plants

There's no single way to do plant magick. Here are some of the most common.

  • Spell components: Add herbs to charm bags, spell jars, amulets, candles, and incense.
  • Living plants: Keep plants growing in your home or garden to encourage growth and well-being in your own life.
  • Divination and observation: Watch how plants behave for omens and signs, or press flowers into your book of shadows.
  • Healing and wellness: Blend herbs into teas, poultices, and fragrant potpourris (only once you've checked their safety notes).

It helps to think of a spell like a recipe. A good cook knows great food comes from quality ingredients combined in the right amounts at the right time. Spells work the same way. Every ingredient should build the energy toward your goal, and everything you add should mesh with everything else.

Just remember the most important ingredient: you. The witch is the key component of any spell.

How to Use Herbs in Your Spellwork

Once you've chosen your herbs, there are a lot of ways to put them to work. Here are some of the most common.

Burning: Burning plant material to release its energy as smoke, traditionally called fumigation, is one of the oldest known methods of spellcasting. It unites all four elements in a single act. Fire transforms the plant, a product of earth and water, into smoke that rides the air and carries your intention through the space. Burning herbs is used for cleansing and to awaken a plant's energy so it can get to work. Commercial incense comes as sticks and cones, but the original format is loose dried herbs ground into a powder with a mortar and pestle and burned on charcoal.

Infusing oils: An infused oil is made by placing dried herbs in a carrier oil and letting the oil take on the plant's properties over time. Always use dried material, since fresh plants hold water and can cause mold. To make one, fill a 4-ounce bottle halfway with carrier oil, add about 2 tablespoons of dried herbs, and top it off with more oil. Shake it daily for the first week, then let it sit somewhere dark for at least six weeks. Woody material may need six to eight weeks. Jojoba is a great carrier because it can stay shelf stable for two years or more. Infused oils are perfect for dressing candles, anointing, and bath oils, and they let you work with plants that don't come as essential oils. A few broken bay leaves in a carrier oil, for example, make an oil for communication, protection baths, or love candles.

Sachets and charm bags: Tuck herbs into a small cloth bag to carry with you or hide around your home. Small is often better, since it's easy to slip into a pocket or pin inside your clothing. Fill most of the bag with cotton or batting and put your intention into the spoonful of herbs you add, along with a stone if you like. Cinquefoil carried in a green sachet, for example, is a traditional way to draw money.

Hanging at doors and thresholds: Hanging herbs at your doorways guards the home against unwanted energy, one of the simplest forms of protection magick. Cinquefoil hung at the front door is a classic protection, and tormentil hung over entrance and bedroom doors has been used to promote family harmony. You can also craft an herbal broom from bundles of protective herbs like rosemary and mugwort and hang it on the door. As the herbs dry in place, they slowly release their protective properties into the home.

Before you use any of these methods, take a moment to connect with the plant itself. Every plant is an ally with its own spirit, not just an ingredient. Hold it, breathe in its scent, notice how it feels, and introduce yourself. Then, as you add it to your working, tell it what you need from it. You can even whisper its job to it: "Rosemary, protect this home" or "Bay, carry my words to the person I'm trying to reach." It's a small step, but it turns a handful of dried leaves into a willing partner in your magick. Be intentional with every plant you work with.

Whatever method you choose, don't skip the last step: empowering your herbs. Plants have their own energy, but many have several uses. Rosemary alone can serve love, purification, protection, and healing. To point a mixture toward one goal, you add your own energy to it, a process also called charging or enchanting. Light a candle in a color that fits your intention, hold the herbs in your hands, and focus fully on your goal. Feel the energy build in your body and flow through your hands into the plants, or speak your intention aloud. When you feel the energy has moved into the mixture, set it down and shake your hands to cut the flow.

One safety note for burning: plants affect the body even when your intent is spiritual, and that includes breathing their smoke. Rue, often recommended for protection, is a recognized abortifacient. If you're pregnant, trying to conceive, nursing, or have breathing problems, check every herb's safety profile before you burn it, bathe in it, or handle it for long periods.

A Few Starter Herbs

You don't need a full apothecary to begin. Just like a cook building a spice rack, start with a few staples that suit the spells you actually want to cast, add more as your practice grows, and keep notes on what works for you. Most of these are probably already in your kitchen.

Rosemary: Protection, purification, memory, and clarity, with ties to both the Sun and Saturn. Don't take it in large doses, and avoid it internally if you're pregnant.

Bay laurel: A Sun herb for success, confidence, and personal power. Avoid it if you're pregnant, and keep it away from pets.

Lavender: A Mercury herb for communication, knowledge, and learning. It's generally safe for internal use, but it's toxic to pets.

Basil: A Mars herb for courage, energy, and strength. Avoid it internally if you're pregnant or nursing, and don't give it to infants or toddlers.

Mugwort: A Moon herb for dreams, intuition, and psychic work. Avoid it internally if you're pregnant, and keep it away from pets.

Rose: The classic flower of Venus and love. The petals are generally safe to consume, but the greens are not, and roses can irritate sensitive skin.

Garden Sage: A Jupiter herb for prosperity and expansion. Avoid it internally if you're pregnant, and don't use it long term or in high doses. One important note: not white sage. White sage is sacred to many Indigenous peoples of the Americas and is a closed practice, so if you're not of Indigenous American descent, please don't use it. It's also been heavily overharvested, which has made it harder for Indigenous communities to find for their own ceremonies. Garden sage is the respectful choice.

Notice how different the safety notes are, even for herbs you'd find in any grocery store. Read the next section before you use any of them.

Kitchen Witchery

If most of those herbs are already in your spice cabinet, you're closer to a working practice than you think. Kitchen witchery is magick woven into everyday life. The home and hearth become your altar, and cooking, cleaning, and brewing tea all become spellwork when you do them with intention. Your ingredients are just as likely to come from the farmer's market as from an occult shop.

Anything you prepare with intention carries the same power as a spell. A loaf of bread baked with a clear goal in mind is every bit as powerful as a carved candle. And because you take the food or drink into your body, kitchen magick can be some of the most intimate and powerful work you'll do.

A few simple ways to start:

  • Stir with intention: Stirring sunwise (clockwise) calls in what you want to draw or manifest. Stirring widdershins (counterclockwise) releases or wards off what you don't want.
  • Cook with your correspondences: Add rosemary for protection, basil for courage, or bay for success, and focus on that goal as you add each one.
  • Try edible flowers: Rose petals, borage, calendula, chive blossoms, and nasturtiums all add color and a bit of magick to a dish. Just make sure you have the right plant. Calendula (Calendula officinalis) is edible, but it's easy to confuse with the common garden marigolds (Tagetes), which are a different plant.

As always, check the safety notes before eating anything, and remember that pregnancy and pets change what's safe.

Safety First, Always

Many plants are toxic, irritate the skin, or aren't safe during pregnancy. Some that are perfectly safe for people are dangerous for cats and dogs. Before you work with any plant, check its safety notes, especially before you:

  • Eat or drink it.
  • Put it on your skin.
  • Burn it as incense or smoke.
  • Use it while pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • Use it around children or pets.

When in doubt, work with a plant symbolically instead of handling it directly. A picture, a dried specimen under glass, or a crystal with the same correspondence can stand in. The same goes for allergies. The symbolic link matters more than physical contact.

And please, don't forage blindly. Plants like hemlock look harmless and are deadly. Never harvest protected or endangered species, and when it comes to dosages, teas, tinctures, or anything you plan to take into your body, check a dedicated herbalism resource or talk to a qualified practitioner first.

Buying Herbs (and What to Look For)

You don't have to grow everything yourself. You can't grow a sandalwood tree in most climates, and buying herbs doesn't make them any less powerful. Most cities have an herb shop or health food store with dried herbs, and plenty of online suppliers ship botanicals from around the world.

Just know that a lot of stores don't refresh their stock very often, so that jar of rosemary might be years old. Look for:

  • Bright colors, not faded or dull.
  • Mostly leaves and flowers, with few stems.
  • A fresh, strong scent.

Skip anything that's mostly stems, discolored, moldy, or showing damage from insects. If an online supplier sends you poor-quality herbs, don't order from them again.

Be extra careful with essential oils. If a bottle of "real jasmine oil" is suspiciously cheap, it's almost certainly synthetic, even if it's labeled "essential." Genuine oils like jasmine, rose, neroli, and chamomile are among the most expensive. Price is one of your best clues, and true essential oils carry plant energy that synthetics can't match.

Harvesting with Respect

If you gather your own herbs, the harvest itself is a magickal act. Plants are living beings, and for most of history every harvest, whether it was headed for the dinner table or the altar, came with prayers, offerings, and recognition of the exchange taking place. The principle underneath it all is reciprocity.

  • Ground yourself first: Take a moment to set your intention before you go out. Some witches go barefoot to connect directly with the earth. Even standing still on bare ground for a few minutes helps.
  • Identify before you cut: Use an illustrated field guide, and be completely sure what you're looking at before you take anything.
  • Ask first: Place your hands around the plant and feel its energy. Speak to it, tell it why you're taking part of its life, and invite it into your work.
  • Give something back: Leave an offering at the base of the plant. Water is welcome anywhere. Other traditions offer oatmeal, honey, or bread. Even a coin shows you're willing to give in exchange for what you take.
  • Take only what you need: Never collect more than 25 percent of a plant's growth. If you need roots, which means taking the whole plant, leave the nearby plants of the same species alone. Dried herbs lose potency over time, so there's no reason to gather more than you'll use in a year.
  • Choose your spot carefully: Don't harvest near highways, polluted water, factories, or industrial sites. And wait until the sun has dried off any rain or dew, or your herbs may mold as they dry.

Many traditions also avoid using iron tools to cut herbs, since iron is believed to interfere with a plant's energy. And if you buy your herbs instead of growing them, you can still honor this practice by blessing new materials when they arrive, since commercial harvesting rarely includes any of this care.

Drying and Storing Your Herbs

Drying locks in a plant's power for long-term storage, so you can work with it long after its season has passed.

To dry fresh herbs, gather them into small, loose bunches and hang them upside down somewhere with good airflow and no direct sunlight. A drying rack works great, but a simple wire hanger does the job too. Leave them alone until they're completely dry. For loose leaves or flowers, you can also spread them on a wooden, ceramic, or steel rack, or keep them in a basket and give it a shake each day until they're dry.

Once they're dry, store them in airtight jars and label everything. Dried herbs lose their potency over time, so plan to use them within about a year.

One more tip if you buy dried herbs: learn what your herbs should look and smell like in their real form. Leaves and flowers usually stay recognizable after processing, but chopped and powdered roots can be nearly impossible to tell apart, and some vendors have been known to swap in cheaper substitutes. When a specific root really matters to your spell, buy it whole and grind it yourself.

Where to Go From Here

Start small. Pick three or four herbs that match the work you want to do, learn their correspondences and safety notes inside and out, and keep a record of every time you use them. A personal herbarium, with pressed specimens, notes, and what you've learned, can become one of the most valuable parts of your grimoire.
